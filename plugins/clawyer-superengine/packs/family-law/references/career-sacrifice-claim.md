# Career-Sacrifice Claim — "I gave up my career / put you through school, then you left"

> **Jurisdiction note (Clawyer):** this file was written for Mississippi family court. Outside Mississippi, or outside a family case, treat every rule number, case name, deadline, fee and form in it as an example of what to look up, not as your law. Family-law steps in it do not apply to other kinds of case. Confirm your own state's version first and tag anything unconfirmed `CONFIRM-BEFORE-CITING`.

> The focused playbook for the spouse who **sacrificed their own career, education, or advancement** (often by staying
> home with the children or working to fund the other's schooling) so the other spouse could earn a degree or build a
> career — and is now divorcing with a permanent income gap. This is a **recurring, winnable, high-value claim** for the
> lower-earning spouse, so the firm treats it as its own thing instead of burying it under "alimony." Builds on
> `references/armstrong-ferguson.md`. Research/drafting support — **not legal advice.**

## The legal reality (Mississippi) — set expectations honestly
- **You cannot divide the degree or the future income as property.** A professional degree/license is **not marital
  property** in MS — no exchange value, can't be sold/transferred, dies with the holder. `[CONFIRM — consistent across
  reputable MS sources, 2026-05-23; national majority rule]` So there is **no separate "educational benefits" award.**
- **The remedy is reimbursement alimony.** *Guy v. Guy*, 736 So. 2d 1042 (Miss. 1999) — a spouse who helped pay for the
  other's college/professional education can be **reimbursed**; reimbursement alimony **vests immediately, is
  non-modifiable, and survives the recipient's remarriage/death.** `[CONFIRM BEFORE CITING — confirm Guy pinpoint vs.
  official So. 2d reporter before filing]`
- **The degree's value still matters indirectly** — the enhanced earning capacity it created drives the **income
  disparity**, **the other side's ability to pay**, and the **standard of living** the marriage reached, all of which feed
  the Armstrong alimony analysis and the Ferguson distribution.

## Stack four tools — don't rely on one
| Tool | What it captures | Strongest when |
|---|---|---|
| **Reimbursement alimony** (*Guy v. Guy*) | What you **paid/contributed financially** toward their education | You have records of tuition, expenses, or working to fund it |
| **Periodic alimony** | The **ongoing income gap** + marital standard of living | Long marriage, large permanent disparity |
| **Rehabilitative alimony** | Your **own re-entry/retraining** to rebuild the career you gave up | You can realistically retrain with a plan |
| **Larger Ferguson share** | Your **homemaker / career-sacrifice as "substantial contribution"** → more than half | You enabled their degree/career by carrying home + child |

These are not either/or — plead them together (alimony in the alternative; property share affirmatively). One issue per
*filing* still holds.

## The offense — QUANTIFY the sacrifice (this is what wins it)
Vague "I gave up a lot" loses; specific numbers win. Build the record:
1. **What you paid toward their education** — tuition, books, fees, living expenses while they studied; the income you
   earned that funded the household during school. (→ reimbursement amount.)
2. **The career you gave up** — your job/title/trajectory **before** the sacrifice; the promotions, raises, seniority, or
   schooling you forwent; **what you would likely be earning today but for the sacrifice.** A **vocational / earning-
   capacity expert** quantifies this foregone capacity. (→ periodic/rehabilitative alimony + need.)
3. **The years out of the workforce** — exact dates you were home/underemployed and why (to enable their degree + raise
   the child). (→ contribution + earning-capacity gap.)
4. **The enhanced earning capacity they gained** — their income before the degree vs. now; the permanent, ever-increasing
   trajectory the degree created. (→ disparity, ability to pay, standard of living.)
5. **The marital standard of living** the degree made possible. (→ Armstrong factor.)
6. **What it freed them to do** — name concretely what your carrying the home/child let them accomplish. (→ Ferguson
   "substantial contribution.")

## Evidence to prove it (route to Evidence + Discovery + Forensic/Vocational)
- Your pre-marriage/early-marriage **employment records, pay history, education** (the trajectory you left).
- **Payment records** for their tuition/expenses; bank records showing you funded the household during school.
- **Their** transcripts, degree, licensure dates, and **income before vs. after** (get via discovery — interrogatories,
  RFP, the UCCR 8.05 financial statement).
- A **vocational/earning-capacity expert** for your foregone earnings (licensed human — the firm scopes and prepares).
- Your testimony + corroborating witnesses on the at-home years and the agreement/expectation behind the sacrifice.

## Honest caveat (build on the strongest footing)
Reimbursement alimony (*Guy*) is strongest where you **financially paid** for the education. If your sacrifice was mostly
**enabling** it — staying home, giving up your own advancement — the power is in **periodic/rehabilitative alimony + the
Armstrong/Ferguson contribution factors**, with reimbursement added for whatever you can show you paid. Don't stake the
whole claim on reimbursement if the proof is really about enabling — name each dollar to the right tool.

## Guardrails
- **No gender-unfairness framing** ("as the husband/wife who stayed home I deserve…") — build it on contributions,
  earning capacity, need, standard of living, and the statutory factors.
- **Fault** (she left after benefiting) is one Armstrong factor only — state it factually, **no revenge/punishment
  framing**, no crime allegations.
- One issue per filing; child by initials; citation discipline (confirm *Guy* + the degree-not-property rule before
  filing); client-protection floor.
