# Note-Taking and the Trial Notebook — baby steps (universal, every state)

The method here is universal. It is not legal advice and names no state's law. The firm walks the person through this
**one small step at a time** (crawl before you walk, `operating-doctrine.md`) — never a wall. Auto-fires whenever a
hearing, trial, deposition, mediation, or GAL meeting is coming up, or the person asks "what do I write down?"

## Why notes win (say this first, plainly)
A calm parent with organized notes looks credible. A parent flipping through loose paper looks lost. Notes are how you
remember the exact words the other side said so you can answer them, and how you prove what happened later. You do not
have to be fast or fancy. You have to be organized. The firm builds the structure for you.

## Step 1 — the one-page hearing sheet (before you go)
The firm prepares, and the person carries, a single page with four boxes:
1. **What I am asking the court for today** (one or two plain sentences).
2. **My three best facts** (the strongest, each tied to a document or exhibit number).
3. **What they will probably say, and my calm answer to each.**
4. **My exhibits in order** (Exhibit 1 = ..., Exhibit 2 = ...).
Print it. It is the page you look at when your mind goes blank.

## Step 2 — the Trial Notebook (a cheap 3-ring binder, tabbed)
The firm tells the person exactly what goes behind each tab; the person just files the printouts:
- **Tab 1 — Today's plan** (the one-page sheet above + the day's checklist).
- **Tab 2 — My filings** (file-stamped copies, newest on top).
- **Tab 3 — Their filings.**
- **Tab 4 — Orders** (every signed order).
- **Tab 5 — Exhibits** (numbered, one per sleeve).
- **Tab 6 — Timeline** (the dated chronology of what happened).
- **Tab 7 — Blank paper** for live notes (Step 3).
Bring it to every hearing. The firm keeps the digital version in order so printing is one click.

## Step 3 — taking notes DURING the hearing (the two-column method)
On the blank paper, draw one line down the middle.
- **Left column: what they said.** Short. Just enough to remember it ("said I never go to doctor appts").
- **Right column: my answer / my question.** What you will say or ask when it is your turn
  ("Exhibit 4 = sign-in sheet, I was there 3/12").
You are not writing everything. You are catching the things you must answer. When the other side or their witness speaks,
the **left column becomes your cross-examination list** (pair it with `courtroom-scripts.md` for how to ask).

## Step 4 — what to write down for the record
- **The exact words** of any ruling the judge says out loud (you will compare it to the written order later — see
  `order-integrity.md`).
- **Any date or deadline** the judge or clerk gives ("file by ___", "next hearing ___"). Circle it.
- **Anything that surprised you** or felt wrong — note it, do not react out loud. Calm parents win.
- If you object or want to, jot the one word why ("hearsay?") — `ASK THE CLERK` or the firm whether it applies; do not
  freeze trying to be a lawyer in the moment.

## Step 5 — right after (in the parking lot, before you forget)
Take five minutes and write: what was decided, what you have to do next and by when, and anything you want the firm to
follow up. Hand it to the firm — it updates the timeline, the deadlines, and the next-action list, and tells you the
single next step.

## Crawl reassurance (the firm says this)
"You do not have to remember all of this. I build the sheet, I tell you what goes in each tab, and during the hearing you
just catch what they say in the left column. One step at a time. You are not doing this alone."
