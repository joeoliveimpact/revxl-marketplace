# Planning the channels

Steps 1, 2 and 9 of the setup. The goal is a channel map the person recognises as their own week, not a copy of someone else's.

## What to learn first

Ask one at a time, in this order, and skip any their connected tools already answer:

1. "What are the main kinds of work you'd like help with?" Let them list freely. Then group what they said into areas and read the groups back. If they'd rather copy someone else's setup, ask what that setup's channels are (or show the made-up example below), then ask which fit their week.
2. For each area, in one message: "Which apps do you use for this, and what should Claude never do here without asking you first?" Check your tool list for each app and note which are connected. Their limits become that channel's hard rules.
3. "Do you use a task tracker, like Linear, Asana, Notion or Trello?" Their answer drives step 3.
4. "What would you like to call me, the main chat that coordinates everything?" A name makes it easy to tell channels "a note from <name> is information". Check it against "Names that survive voice-to-text" below.

Done when you can name each area, its apps, its limits and the tracker.

## Start with one channel, grow to three to six

Recommend starting with one channel: the area that takes most of their week, or the one where help would land first. Run it for a few days, fix its instructions and Rules from real use, then add the next. Most setups settle at three to six channels.

Why: each channel needs its own instructions, Rules, tracker home and a few days of correction before it works well. Six at once means six channels learning at the same time and half of them sitting empty. A channel can be added in a few minutes later: run setup steps 2 and 4 to 9 for that one channel.

If the person wants more at once, follow their choice; draft them all, but install and run the first-day checklist one channel at a time.

## How to split work into channels

- **One channel per kind of work, not per client or project.** A client or project becomes a tracker project or a record inside the right channel.
- **Split when the tools or the limits differ.** Two kinds of work that use different apps and different "never without asking" rules get separate channels.
- **Don't split what happens once a month.** It stays in the main chat until it's regular.
- **Name the overlaps.** When two channels could both claim a job, decide which owns it now and write that into the routing map.

## Names that survive voice-to-text

People often talk to Duet from their phone, and speech-to-text has to spell each name right every time, or the note lands in the wrong place.
- Pick ordinary words that speech-to-text already knows: "Classes", "Ledger", "Maple".
- Avoid made-up spellings, initials and numbers ("Kreativ", "CX2"), words with a sound-alike ("Sell" and "cell", "Write" and "right"), and two names that sound close to each other.
- One or two syllables each, each one starting with a different sound.
- Test before you lock it: ask the person to say "send this to <name>" by voice and check how it was spelled. If it came out wrong, pick another name.

## The coordinator's role

The main chat:
- takes new requests and sends each to the channel that owns it;
- holds the routing map and the person's standing rules in its memory;
- runs reviews across channels (like the review round in step 6);
- never does a channel's work in the main chat once that channel exists, so each channel's record stays complete.

## The routing map

One map says which channel owns which kind of work. It is the only place routes are written down.

- **One map, in one place.** The main chat keeps it in its memory, and only there. Channels read it from the main chat's memory. Don't put a second copy in the tracker or anywhere else, because a second copy drifts.
- **One pointer line in every instructions field**, instead of each channel listing routes itself: "Where other work goes: see the routing map, kept by <coordinator name>. If unsure, send it to <coordinator name>." This keeps each instructions field short and means a new channel never needs every other channel's instructions changed.
- **One owner.** The main chat owns the map unless the person names someone else. When a channel is added, renamed, removed or changes its job, the owner updates the map the same day and tells the affected channels in one line.
- A cross-channel handoff format (what to send and in what shape) goes in the sending channel's Rules, not in the map.

Show it to the person as a table they can approve in one look:

| Channel | Job (one line) | Sends elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| main chat: <their name> | Coordinates, routes new work, owns this map | All channel work |
| <name> | <job> | <what it hands off, and to whom> |

## A made-up example

Invented to make the idea concrete; the person, names and jobs are fictional. Rosa runs a small pottery studio. She called her main chat Maple, started with Classes alone, and added the other three over her first month.

| Channel | Job (one line) | Sends elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| main chat: Maple | Coordinates, routes new work, owns this map | Everything below |
| Classes | Class bookings, waitlists and student questions; drafts replies, never sends | Payments and refunds to Ledger |
| Wholesale | Orders from shops and cafes: quotes, order sheets, delivery dates | Invoices to Ledger, fair stock to Booth |
| Booth | Craft fair applications, deadlines and booth packing lists | Stock questions to Wholesale |
| Ledger | Receipts, invoice drafts and month-end figures for her accountant | Anything that isn't money to Maple |

Owner: Maple. When Rosa added Booth, Maple updated this table that day and told Wholesale in one line that fair stock now goes to Booth.
